The first problem in a dating business is not software. It is giving each early member a realistic chance of meeting someone relevant.

A beautiful product with no local density feels broken. That is why I would plan the first hundred useful members before building the hundredth feature.

## Start where you can reach people

A good first market is small enough to understand and close enough to reach without a large advertising budget.

It might be a city, professional group, interest community or existing audience. The important question is not whether the niche sounds clever. It is whether you can speak to real people inside it, learn what they currently do and invite a credible first cohort.

Do not ask only whether they “like the idea”. Ask about their last experience:

- Which existing app or introduction method did they use?
- Where did it waste time or feel unsafe?
- What information helped them decide to reply?
- Why did they stop using it?
- What would make them recommend a focused alternative?

Past behaviour is usually better evidence than polite enthusiasm.

## Choose how the marketplace starts

Opening registration to the whole world is not a launch strategy. Pick a method that protects early relevance:

- invite members in one location;
- open by weekly cohort;
- use a waiting list until both sides of the market are represented;
- begin with curated introductions;
- partner with an existing community;
- offer events or useful editorial content before relying on matching alone.

The right method depends on the product. A private matchmaking service may begin with interviews and manual introductions. A niche community may begin with conversations and events. Automation comes after you understand the work worth automating.

## Define activation honestly

A completed registration is not necessarily an activated member. Choose a behaviour close to value, such as:

- completing the profile fields required for a credible recommendation;
- viewing a useful set of relevant people;
- accepting a curated introduction;
- starting a safe, two-way conversation;
- returning after receiving a relevant notification.

Measure where people stop. Improve that journey before adding more acquisition.

## Understand unit economics early

Write down the costs that grow with usage:

- payment fees;
- transactional email and SMS;
- media storage and delivery;
- maps or location services;
- identity or fraud checks;
- AI API calls;
- moderation and member support;
- refunds and disputes.

An AI feature that looks inexpensive in development can become a large variable cost at scale. Estimate the cost per active member and per paying member. Then decide whether the feature improves retention, safety or revenue enough to justify it.

## Own the differentiator, connect the rest

The relationship model, matching rules and member experience are core business logic. Keep them understandable and under your control.

Payments, email and notifications are supporting capabilities. External APIs can be the right choice when they reduce operational burden, but keep the provider boundary explicit. Test request construction, response handling and failure behaviour so a vendor change does not silently break the product.

## Use the launch as a learning system

Each week, review a small set of evidence:

- relevant new members, not only total signups;
- profile completion;
- qualified introductions or conversations;
- report and block themes;
- cancellations and refund reasons;
- acquisition source quality;
- support workload;
- cost per active and paying member.
