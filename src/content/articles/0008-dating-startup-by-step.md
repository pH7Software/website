There is no twelve-step formula that guarantees a successful dating business. There is, however, a sensible order for reducing risk before the software, marketing and support costs become difficult to reverse.

This is the order I would use today.

## 1. Choose the first people you can actually reach

Start with a focused audience or market where you can speak to real potential members. A niche is useful when it creates shared context and a practical acquisition path. A clever label without access to the people inside it is not enough.

## 2. Study the current workaround

Interview people about the last time they tried to meet someone through an app, community, event, matchmaker or friend. Look for repeated friction, not abstract feature requests.

Write down which alternative they used, why they chose it, where trust weakened and what made them leave.

## 3. Define the member promise

Complete this sentence:

> We help **this specific group** make **this specific kind of connection** through **this meaningful difference**.

The promise should guide profile questions, discovery, pricing and the launch message. If it does not change a product decision, it is still too generic.

## 4. Decide how the first market reaches density

Choose a city, cohort, community or invitation model. Decide how many relevant people need to be active before discovery becomes useful.

You may need manual curation, events or staged invitations at first. That is not a failure of automation. It is a way to learn which work deserves automation.

## 5. Set trust and privacy boundaries

Decide the minimum age, supported markets, profile visibility, location precision, reporting process, blocking behaviour and moderator response.

Get market-specific legal and privacy advice before launch. An open-source application cannot decide which rules apply to your business and users.

## 6. Choose the smallest complete journey

The first version should let a relevant person:

1. understand the promise;
2. join and complete the information required for a useful result;
3. discover or receive a relevant connection;
4. communicate safely;
5. report a problem or leave.

Everything else competes with making this journey credible.

## 7. Build on pH7Builder

Use the working dating and social domains rather than generating another empty application:

```console
composer create-project ph7software/ph7builder my-dating-app
```

Read the project guide before modifying the source. Map requirements to existing behaviour, then isolate the customer-specific extension. Keep the product blueprint next to the code.

## 8. Connect supporting services deliberately

Choose email, payment, storage, location and notification providers based on the launch market and operating capacity. Do not make one provider part of the core dating logic.

For every API, record the variable cost, credentials, failure behaviour and replacement boundary.

## 9. Prove the critical behaviour

Write outcome-focused tests for matching and business rules. Use browser tests for member interactions. Run the complete critical journey against the deployed environment, including email and scheduled work.

Create backups and prove a restore. A successful backup task alone is not enough.

## 10. Launch with a cohort you can observe

Invite a small group that fits the actual audience. Watch where people hesitate, what they misunderstand and whether the suggested connections feel credible.

Make it easy to reach a human when trust is involved.

## 11. Review useful evidence each week

Track activation, qualified connections, two-way conversations, reports, cancellations, support themes, acquisition quality and variable cost. Total registration count can hide a marketplace that is not helping anyone.

## 12. Expand only after the reason is clear

When one focused market works, document why. Then test whether that reason survives a new city, audience or acquisition channel.

Do not scale a vague result. Do not franchise a process nobody can yet explain. Keep the product decisions, repository history and operating evidence clear enough that another person—or another agent—can continue the work.
